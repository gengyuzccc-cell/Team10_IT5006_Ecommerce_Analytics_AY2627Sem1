\section*{Executive Summary}
\phantomsection\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Executive Summary}
\label{sec:executive}

Every order on Olist --- a Brazilian marketplace that places small sellers on
large retail platforms and manages fulfilment for them --- carries a promise to
a customer: the delivery date shown at checkout. Its public dataset (99{,}441
orders in nine relational tables, 2016--2018) shows what keeping that promise is
worth to the customer: 9.2\% of customers whose parcel arrives on time rate the
order one or two stars, against 54.0\% of those whose parcel is late. Box~1
follows one of them, from checkout to a one-star review written 5.8 days before
the parcel arrived. The project asks two questions at two moments of the
customer's order, each for the team that can act on the answer.
\textbf{Problem 1, at checkout} (operations): how long will this order take, and
will it miss the date the customer is shown? \textbf{Problem 2, at delivery}
(customer experience): will this customer rate the order one or two stars, while
there is still time to help?

Three disciplines run through the work. Leakage is defined per prediction moment
and enforced by a guard --- one forbidden column lifts a late-order classifier's
ROC-AUC from 0.589 to 1.000. The split is chronological,
and the test window (orders placed 30 June to 30 August 2018) was scored once,
for models already chosen. Three model families climb from their simplest
variant, each rung compared with the one below.

The lead-time regression is off by \textbf{3.90 days} on average, 41\% less than
a constant fitted on the past and 60\% less than the platform's own date;
compared with that date it gives a late-order flag at 2.9 times the no-skill
floor, where the best classifier trained on the label reaches 1.2 times. The
low-review model reaches ROC-AUC 0.749 and PR-AUC 0.414, \textbf{3.75 times its
floor}; at its own moment --- each day's delivered parcels whose customer has not
yet rated them --- its top ten find 26 future 1--2 star reviewers in 100, 3.2
times chance.

The recommendations follow the customer's order and are counted first in
customers (Table~\ref{tab:customer}). \emph{At checkout}, show the
forecast plus an eight-day margin chosen on the validation months (the forecast
alone is exceeded by 31.9\% of deliveries): on the held-out window
the date is broken for 25 in 1{,}000 delivered orders instead of 74, for a
quoted wait 1.1 days longer. \emph{When the promised day ends without a
delivery} (69.5 in 1{,}000 orders placed; 55\% of those who rated the order only
afterwards gave it 1--2 stars), tell the customer that day --- an apology, the
status, a new date --- and ask every customer for the review once the order is
complete. \emph{At delivery}, work the daily list from the top
with a replacement or refund to offer, since customers whose parcel arrived
complain about wrong or missing items, quality and damage. Expediting freight on
the checkout flag is not recommended. Money comes second, and on assumed costs,
since Olist publishes none: at R\$5 a contact and R\$40 a low rating, the
promised-date message pays if it turns around about one unhappy customer in
four, the delivery-day call one in two --- an effect no historical data can
show, which a randomised pilot of five to seven weeks would measure
(Section~\ref{sec:pilot}).

Everything is deployed and reproducible. A Streamlit application at
\url{https://team10-it5006-olist.streamlit.app} opens on one customer's order,
serves the three models, replays any held-out day blind and then with its
outcomes, and labels each figure observed, assumed, derived or planned. The repository
regenerates every number in this document, \checkcount{} validation checks tie
models, tables and text together, and the feedback on Phases 1 and 2 and on the
presentation of 5 November 2026 --- including where each number on the slides
comes from --- is answered in Appendices~\ref{app:feedback} and
\ref{app:provenance}.
