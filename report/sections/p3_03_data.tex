\section{Dataset and Preprocessing}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Nine tables, one row per order}

The dataset \citep{olist2018dataset} comprises nine related CSV tables, roughly
1.55 million rows; \code{orders} is the spine, at 99{,}441 rows, one per order,
and the other eight attach to it directly or through \code{order\_items}
(Figure~\ref{fig:datamodel}; Table~\ref{tab:dictionary} in
Appendix~\ref{app:dictionary} is the full data dictionary). Three tables are
\textbf{one-to-many} against orders, and joining them naively is the first trap
the data sets: a direct orders--items--payments merge yields 118{,}434 rows
rather than 99{,}441, a 19\% inflation that silently reweights every mean
towards large baskets. Each child table is therefore aggregated to one row per
order --- counts, sums and the attributes of the most expensive item --- and
the grain is asserted after the join. All data come from these nine tables, the
brief's only data source. Outside information enters in two places only, each
labelled where it is used: two news reports date the May 2018 strike, and the
costs of Section~\ref{sec:cost} are assumptions, not data.

The second trap is identity. \code{customer\_id} is regenerated for every
order, so it is an order key, not a person: 99{,}441 distinct values map to only
\textbf{96{,}096} \code{customer\_unique\_id} values. Grouping on the wrong one
reports zero repeat buyers; the right one finds 2{,}997 people (3.1\%) who
ordered again. \code{customer\_unique\_id} is carried throughout.

Missingness is \textbf{structural rather than random}: 97.0\% of orders reach
status \emph{delivered}, and the 2.98\% with no delivery timestamp are orders
whose delivery has not happened, so their delivery targets are undefined and
excluded rather than imputed. Elsewhere the data is remarkably complete: 99.2\%
of orders carry a review and product attributes are missing for under 2\% of
products. The remaining issues --- reused \code{review\_id} values and orders
with several reviews (keyed on \code{order\_id}, earliest kept), free-text
comments, written with the rating and so never features
(Section~\ref{sec:reviews} reads them descriptively), a million geolocation rows
collapsed to a median coordinate per postcode prefix, products without a
category --- are recorded with their evidence as \code{CLEANING\_DECISIONS} in
\code{src/data\_load.py} and tabulated in Table~\ref{tab:quality}
(Appendix~\ref{app:dictionary}).
Numeric imputation (median) happens inside each model pipeline, never on the
frame.

\subsection{Analysis window and the three-way split}
\label{sec:split}

The raw file spans September 2016 to October 2018, but the first four months
are a pilot contributing 329 orders against a 2018 run rate above 6{,}000 a
month, and the final weeks are truncated mid-cycle
(Figure~\ref{fig:coverage}), so the analysis is restricted to \textbf{orders
placed from 1 January 2017 to 30 August 2018}, leaving \textbf{99{,}091} orders.

Each period of the split has exactly one job. \textbf{Train} (1 January 2017 to
29 April 2018, 73{,}008 orders) fits every model, with expanding-window
cross-validation inside it tuning hyperparameters. \textbf{Validation}
(30 April to 29 June 2018, 13{,}156) chooses between tuned models, sets every
decision threshold and fits the margin-to-risk calibration. \textbf{Test}
(30 June to 30 August 2018, 62 purchase days, 12{,}927) is scored once, at the
end, for models already chosen, and is the held-out window the deployed
application replays (Section~\ref{sec:deployment}). The split is chronological rather than
stratified-random because the target drifts (Section~\ref{sec:eda}) and a
random split would train on the future; the positive rate of each period is
reported instead --- late orders 8.8\%, 5.0\% and 7.4\% of the training,
validation and test periods, low reviews 15.8\%, 11.4\% and 11.0\%. A purely
temporal cut can still put one person's orders on each side, so the \textbf{227} straddling buyers are removed
from a clean test subset used in Section~\ref{sec:results-review}.

\subsection{Leakage: one rule per prediction moment}
\label{sec:leakage}

Leakage is defined per \emph{prediction moment}, because what is known depends
on when the model runs. \emph{At checkout} nothing about fulfilment or the
customer's opinion exists, so delivery dates and everything derived from them,
the review and its timestamps, \code{order\_approved\_at},
\code{shipping\_limit\_date} and \code{order\_status} are forbidden. \emph{At
delivery} only the review and its timestamps remain forbidden: the delivery
outcome is now history, and the brief lists ``delivery performance'' among that
problem's features. \code{assert\_no\_leakage(features, regime)} enforces the set
before any model is fitted, is tested in both directions and raises on an
unknown regime.

\code{order\_estimated\_delivery\_date} is admissible at checkout: the customer
sees that promise when ordering, so it precedes the outcome and encodes what
Olist's routing knew --- a useful feature and (Section~\ref{sec:results-reg}) a
poor prediction. Getting the regime wrong is measurable: one forbidden column,
the realised delivery time, lifts the late-delivery model's ROC-AUC from 0.589
to \textbf{1.000} (Figure~\ref{fig:leakage}) --- not a result but the target
restated.
