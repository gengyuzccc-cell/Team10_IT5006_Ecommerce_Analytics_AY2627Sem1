\section{Problem Definition and Stakeholder Context}
\label{sec:problem}

Phase 1 left one finding standing above the rest: a late delivery raises the
probability of a 1--2 star review from 9.2\% to 54.0\%, a 5.9-fold increase
monotone in how late the parcel is. Phase 2 turns that sentence into a pair of
predictions placed at two points in an order's life.

The brief permits two problems or one framed twice; we take two, because they are
halves of one decision chain. Operations acts before the parcel ships; customer
experience acts after it arrives and before the customer writes anything.
Problem 1's output is, in substance, Problem 2's most important input.

Problem 1 is the brief's \emph{dual framing}: one underlying quantity, two
outputs, so the late-order flag \emph{is} the regression's answer compared
against the promised date. Five classifiers trained directly on the binary label
run alongside as a control arm, testing whether the flag needs a model of its
own; Section~\ref{sec:results-late} reports that comparison, including a
disagreement between the selection protocol and the forward window that is itself
one of this report's findings.

\paragraph{How the stakeholders act on the output.}
A prediction is only worth producing if someone changes a decision because of
it, and the Milestone 1 feedback asked what the operations team would actually
\emph{do} with a lead-time estimate. Table~\ref{tab:levers} answers for both
teams, and shapes the evaluation: the cheap operations levers apply to every
order and are judged on the regression's accuracy; the expensive one, and all
of customer experience's work, are rationed by capacity and are judged on what
the top-$K$ of a ranked list delivers (Section~\ref{sec:results-capacity}).

\paragraph{Targets.} All three come from the provided tables alone.
\emph{Lead time} is the time from \code{order\_purchase\_timestamp} to
\code{order\_delivered\_customer\_date}, in days, defined for the 97.0\% of
orders that reached the customer. \emph{Late} is delivery strictly after
\code{order\_estimated\_delivery\_date}, which carries no time component, so
lateness is accurate to about a day. \emph{Low review} is \code{review\_score}
$\le 2$, defined for the 99.2\% carrying a review. Undefined rows are \code{NaN}
and excluded, never imputed to a negative label: the event has not happened
rather than gone missing, and a silent customer is not a satisfied one.

\paragraph{What the review score measures, and what it does not.}
Phase 1's review treated the service-quality literature's \emph{perceived
quality} and a platform rating as one construct; they are not. Perceived
service quality is measured by survey instruments such as E-S-QUAL
\citep{parasuraman2005esqual} and e-LSQ \citep{rao2011elsq}: self-reported,
retrospective, subject to recall bias. A star rating is a behavioural trace
left by the customers who choose to write one, and the J-shaped distribution of
ratings -- 57.8\% five stars here, with a second mode at one star -- is the
signature of acquisition and under-reporting biases: satisfied buyers
self-select into purchasing, and only strongly moved customers into reviewing
\citep{hu2009jshaped, hu2017selfselection}. A rating also moves with the
product, its price and the seller's communication
\citep{dellarocas2003digitization}. The bridge Phase 1 lacked is
\citet{deshpande2023logistics}, who show on transactional data rather than
surveys that logistics performance moves the platform rating itself. Problem 2
therefore predicts the \emph{rating}, the observable the customer-experience
team is measured on, and claims nothing about latent satisfaction; the
consequence, that part of the residual is unobserved product and seller
effects, is carried into Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\paragraph{Success criteria, registered in advance.}
Expected ranges for all six headline metrics were written into
\code{config.EXPECTED\_BANDS} \emph{before} any Phase 2 model was tuned, from the
Phase 1 results and untuned scoping probes (Appendix~\ref{app:bands}). They are
the targets the work is judged against and they are a leak alarm --- because here
the signature of leakage is not a subtle improvement but an implausible one, as
Phase 1 showed by adding one forbidden column to the late-delivery model and
watching ROC-AUC go from 0.589 to 1.000. A result landing far \emph{above} its
band is investigated before it is reported. All six landed inside; none above.

The ceiling deserves stating as plainly as the target. Lateness here is volatile
rather than trending, 1.4\% to 21.4\% month to month, and Phase 2 re-examined
where that volatility comes from after the Milestone 1 feedback found the
Phase 1 attribution unsupported (Appendix~\ref{app:lateness},
Figure~\ref{fig:lateness}). Black Friday 2017 is a genuine spike (14.3\%). The
largest peaks, February and March 2018 (16.0\% and 21.4\%), are a network-wide
carrier-side slowdown -- transit from carrier handoff to doorstep lengthened
from 8.7 to 13.2 days while sellers' handling time did not move -- that
coincided with the platform \emph{tightening} its promised window by about four
days. The truckers' strike of 21 May to 1 June 2018 \citep{bloomberg2018strike,
tecnoblog2018correios} is real but invisible at monthly resolution: orders
placed the week before it and caught in transit were 16.0\% late, and the
platform then lengthened its promise from 19.5 to 36.8 days, which is why June
2018 is the quietest month in the series. None of these shocks is an attribute
of an order, so nothing available at checkout can anticipate them. A
late-delivery classifier reporting 95\% accuracy here would be the
majority-class rule or a leak.
