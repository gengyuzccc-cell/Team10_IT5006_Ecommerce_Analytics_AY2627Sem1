\section{Exploratory Data Analysis}

\subsection{Dataset overview and data model}

The dataset comprises \textbf{nine} related CSV tables, roughly 1.45 million
rows; \code{orders} is the spine, at 99{,}441 rows -- one per order -- and the
other eight attach to it directly or through \code{order\_items}
(Figure~\ref{fig:datamodel}; Table~\ref{tab:dictionary} gives the full data
dictionary).

Three tables are \textbf{one-to-many} against orders, and joining them naively is
the first trap the data sets: a direct orders--items--payments merge yields
118{,}437 rows rather than 99{,}441, a 19\% inflation that silently reweights
every mean towards large orders, so we aggregate each child table to one row per
order first, then join and assert the grain.

The second trap is identity. \code{customer\_id} is regenerated for every order,
so it is an order key, not a person: 99{,}441 distinct values map to only
\textbf{96{,}096} \code{customer\_unique\_id} values. Grouping on the wrong one
reports zero repeat buyers; the right one finds 2{,}997 people (3.1\%) who
ordered again, one of them 17 times. We carry \code{customer\_unique\_id}
throughout and split on it (Section~\ref{sec:candidates}).

\subsection{Data quality}

Table~\ref{tab:quality} records every issue and our decision, as implemented in
\code{src/data\_load.py}.

Missingness here is \textbf{structural rather than random}: 97.0\% of orders reach
status \emph{delivered}, and the 2.98\% with no delivery timestamp are orders
whose delivery has not happened, so their delivery targets are undefined and
excluded rather than imputed. Elsewhere the data is remarkably complete: 99.2\%
of orders carry a review and product attributes are missing for under 2\% of
products.

Two decisions shape the window. The first four months are a pilot contributing 326
orders against a 2018 run rate above 6{,}000 per month, and the final weeks are
truncated mid-cycle, so we restrict the analysis to \textbf{2017-01-01 to
2018-08-31}, leaving 99{,}091 orders (Figure~\ref{fig:coverage}).

\subsection{Temporal patterns: growth, seasonality and drift}
\label{sec:temporal}

Monthly order volume rises roughly \textbf{eight-fold} across the window, from
about 800 orders in January 2017 to 6{,}000--7{,}500 a month through 2018,
peaking at \textbf{Black Friday, November 2017} with 7{,}544 orders, 1.5 times
the median month (Figure~\ref{fig:volume}a). Purchasing is weekday-heavy, peaks
at 16:00, and is 22\% weekend (Figure~\ref{fig:rhythm}).

The most consequential temporal finding is not seasonality but \textbf{drift}.
Mean lead time falls from 16.9 days in February 2018 to 7.7 in August 2018 --
Olist materially improved fulfilment over twenty months
(Figure~\ref{fig:volume}b) -- and across the chronological split used later the
mean falls by \textbf{5.34 days} between training and test.

Lateness behaves differently: it is \textbf{volatile rather than trending},
swinging from 1.4\% (June 2018) to 21.4\% (March 2018). Black Friday is a real
spike (14.3\%), but the largest peaks are February--March 2018 (16.0\%, 21.4\%),
a carrier-side slowdown that coincided with a tightened promise. The truckers'
strike of 21 May--1 June 2018 \citep{bloomberg2018strike, tecnoblog2018correios}
shows only at weekly resolution (orders caught in transit were 16.0\% late)
because Olist then lengthened its promise from 19.5 to 36.8 days, which is why
June is the trough; shocks of this kind are what no checkout-time feature can
anticipate.

Together these facts set the evaluation strategy. Under a level shift this size,
$R^2$ against a baseline fitted on the past is negative even for a useful model,
so we report \textbf{mean absolute error} as the headline regression metric
\citep{hyndman2006accuracy}, and treat periodic refitting as a Phase 2
requirement rather than an optimisation \citep{gama2014drift}.

\subsection{Customers, sellers, products and geography}

The marketplace is geographically \textbf{lopsided}, and that asymmetry drives the
delivery problem: \textbf{59.7\%} of sellers sit in S\~{a}o Paulo state against
\textbf{42.0\%} of customers, only 35.7\% of orders stay within the customer's own
state, and the median customer--seller distance is \textbf{434\,km}
(Figure~\ref{fig:geography}). Distance is the strongest checkout-time correlate of
lead time ($r = 0.39$), ahead of the platform's own estimate ($r = 0.38$). The
full matrix (Figure~\ref{fig:correlations}) adds three facts the models inherit:
promise and distance correlate 0.52, so the promise is largely a distance rule;
freight tracks weight (0.64) rather than price; and review score correlates
$-0.33$ with lead time and essentially zero with price or basket size -- the
delivery, not the purchase, drives the rating.

Demand is long-tailed across 74 categories, the top ten covering two-thirds of
orders; the typical order is small (median item value R\$86.90) but
\textbf{freight is not a rounding error} (median R\$17.17, 22\% of item value),
and payment is characteristically Brazilian: 75.4\% credit card, 51.4\% in
instalments (Figure~\ref{fig:products}).

\subsection{Reviews, and the relationship that dominates}
\label{sec:reviews}

Review scores are severely imbalanced -- \textbf{77.1\%} are four or five stars --
exactly the imbalance the brief warns about; the interesting structure is what
produces the minority.

A late delivery raises the probability of a 1--2 star review from \textbf{9.2\%}
to \textbf{54.0\%}, a \textbf{5.9-fold} increase, monotone in how late the parcel
is (Figure~\ref{fig:reviews}). This is the clearest relationship in the dataset,
and it reproduces at order level the logistics--rating link
\citet{deshpande2023logistics} establish across 15 million orders. It also has a
methodological consequence: a model predicting review score \emph{at checkout}
cannot see the variable that matters most.

\subsection{Candidate problem exploration}
\label{sec:candidates}

We examined three candidates. All probes use \code{scikit-learn} pipelines on a
chronological split (train to 2018-04-30, validation to 2018-06-30, test
thereafter) additionally \textbf{grouped on \code{customer\_unique\_id}}, removing
the 227 buyers who would otherwise appear on both sides.\footnote{\textbf{Note on
splitting.} We split by time because the target drifts (Section~\ref{sec:temporal})
and a random split lets a model learn from the future; 2{,}997 people ordered more
than once, so a temporal cut alone can still put one person's orders on both sides
-- the brief's \code{customer\_id} warning in concrete form -- hence the grouping,
and a test subset of customers never seen in training.}

\paragraph{Leakage depends on when the model runs.}
Rather than one global rule we define a forbidden set per \textbf{prediction
moment}. \emph{At checkout} nothing about fulfilment or the customer's opinion
exists: delivery dates, review scores and even \code{order\_approved\_at} are
future. \emph{At delivery} the parcel has arrived and the review is not yet
written, so the delivery outcome is history and admissible -- the brief itself
lists ``delivery performance'' among this problem's features. The guard
\code{assert\_no\_leakage(features, regime)} enforces the right set.

The cost of getting this wrong is measurable: adding one forbidden column, the
realised delivery time, lifts the late-delivery model's ROC-AUC from \textbf{0.589
to 1.000} (Figure~\ref{fig:leakage}) -- not a result but the target restated.

\paragraph{Candidate 1 --- delivery performance, framed twice (primary).}
One question answered as a regression on lead time and a classification of late
versus on-time, satisfying the brief's requirement for both task types from one
coherent problem. Stakeholder: operations, which would use the two outputs at
checkout to re-quote the promised date, to pre-warn buyers whose orders are
flagged, and to reserve expediting, the costly action, for the top of the
ranked list.

The regression works. Gradient boosting reaches \textbf{MAE 3.89 days} against
6.65 for a constant fitted on the training period, a \textbf{41.5\%} reduction,
with ridge at 4.61 days in between. Plain least squares, the simpler starting
point, is unusable here -- the design matrix is rank-deficient, so its
coefficients explode -- and ridge at $\alpha = 1$ is the minimum regularisation
that repairs it. Olist's own promise is a \emph{worse} point forecast than the
constant (MAE 9.79 days): a promised date is a commercial decision, padded
because a broken promise costs returns and retention \citep{cui2024promise} --
a useful feature, not a useful prediction.

The classification is weak, and Section~\ref{sec:temporal} explains why: balanced
accuracy 0.580, PR-AUC 0.121 against a 0.074 floor. Crucially, \textbf{always
predicting ``on time'' scores 0.926 accuracy} while catching not a single late
order -- the exact trap the brief describes.

\paragraph{Candidate 2 --- low review score, predicted at delivery (secondary).}
The strongest classifier we found: PR-AUC \textbf{0.402} against a 0.110 floor
(3.6$\times$), balanced accuracy \textbf{0.692} -- and identical on customers never
seen in training (PR-AUC 0.403), so it is not memorising buyers. The prediction
moment is what makes it work: the same target predicted \emph{at checkout} reaches
only PR-AUC 0.212. Stakeholder: customer experience, with a concrete action --
flag an at-risk order for outreach before the review lands.

\paragraph{Candidate 3 --- repeat purchase within 30 days (rejected).}
The base rate is only 1.8\% -- 96.9\% of customers appear exactly once -- and
the probe reaches balanced accuracy 0.517, essentially chance; and
\textbf{right-censoring destroys the evaluation}, since at the more natural
90-day horizon \emph{no} labelled orders remain in the test window, a structural
limit of a 21-month extract. Table~\ref{tab:checklist} scores all three.

\subsection{Key insights}

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep0pt\parsep0pt\topsep2pt
\item A naive join of the three one-to-many tables inflates the data 19\% and
biases every average.
\item \code{customer\_id} is an order key, not a person: the difference between
0\% and 3.1\% repeat buyers.
\item Delivery performance \textbf{drifts}: lead time halves, so MAE, not $R^2$,
is the honest metric.
\item Lateness is \textbf{volatile, not predictable} (1.4--21.4\% monthly), which
bounds the late-delivery classifier near 1.6$\times$ the no-skill floor.
\item A late delivery raises the 1--2 star rate from 9.2\% to 54.0\%, the
strongest relationship in the data.
\item \textbf{The prediction moment is a design decision, not a detail}: it nearly
doubles achievable PR-AUC one way, and inflates ROC-AUC to a meaningless 1.000 the
other.
\item \textbf{Expected Phase 2 ceiling, stated in advance}: balanced accuracy in
the high 60s for Candidate 2, MAE of 3.5--4 days for Candidate 1; a late
classifier at 95\% accuracy would be a leak. \code{src/report\_stats.py}
regenerates every number quoted here.
\end{enumerate}
