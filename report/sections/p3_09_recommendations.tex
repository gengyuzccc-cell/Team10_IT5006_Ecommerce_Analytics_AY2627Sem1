\section{Business Recommendations and Impact}
\label{sec:insight}

\subsection{What the models rely on}

Importance is measured by permutation on the held-out window rather than by
impurity on the training set, which inflates high-cardinality features
\citep{strobl2007importance} (Figure~\ref{fig:importance}). \textbf{Problem 2
has one dominant driver, and it is actionable.} How late the parcel was carries
0.206 of PR-AUC on its own, how long it took a further 0.074, the size of the
order 0.045; the top two account for \textbf{76.9\%} of total positive
importance, which is why a single depth-limited tree captured 96\% of the
achievable signal --- good news for customers, because the platform already
measures that driver and can act on it. \textbf{Problem 1A has no single point
of failure}: no feature costs more than 0.034 days of MAE, because permutation
understates correlated predictors such as the promise, the route histories and
the distance.

\subsection{From a probability to a decision: what is observed, what is assumed}
\label{sec:cost}

Olist publishes no costs, so every reais figure below rests on
\emph{assumed} ones (\code{config.COSTS}): R\$5 for a contact, and R\$40 for a
1--2 star rating a contact turns around --- a stand-in for the damage low ratings
do to seller sales on another marketplace \citep{deshpande2023logistics}, not a
measured cost. Nor can the customer's own return price it: 98\% of first-time
buyers order nothing within 180 days of delivery (Appendix~\ref{app:customer}). What is \emph{observed} is how many customers a
contact reaches and the share who go on to rate 1--2 stars, its precision; the
share a contact turns around is unknown until the pilot measures it. A contact
pays when precision times that share exceeds $5/40 = 0.125$
\citep{elkan2001cost}, so Table~\ref{tab:cost} \emph{derives} the share each
contact must turn around.

If every contact turned its customer around, the promised-date message would be
worth R\$1{,}056 per 1{,}000 reviewed orders and the delivery-day call R\$229; if
one in four did, R\$30 and $-$R\$102 (Appendix~\ref{app:customer} varies the
costs and the effect). The slides' R\$775--1{,}466 values the review-risk list over
all reviewed orders at a threshold, 0.11, read off the test window
(Figure~\ref{fig:threshold}), with every contact assumed to work: R\$1{,}466
counts every listed customer who went on to rate 1--2 stars, R\$775 leaves out those
who rated a late parcel before it arrived. The ten-a-day list's 35-in-100
share of the rest is worth R\$433, and three fifths of the threshold
list's gross value comes from customers whose order never came or who rated a
late parcel before it arrived --- the promised-date message's customers
(Appendix~\ref{app:provenance}).
\textbf{The late flag does not pay for an expensive action}: expediting at an assumed R\$12
needs precision 0.300, a conservative bar (Appendix~\ref{app:provenance}), and
the flag peaks at \textbf{0.274} --- informative (ROC-AUC 0.782), but only for
near-free actions: a better date, an early word.

\subsection{Recommendations along the customer's order}
\label{sec:stakeholders}

\textbf{1. At checkout: a date the customer can rely on} (operations). Quote the
forecast plus a margin, not the forecast: it estimates the typical delivery, and
on its own it is exceeded by 31.9\% of deliveries. The margin is the smallest,
in tenths of a day, whose broken-promise rate on the validation months is no
worse than the platform's own, with the date rounded up to a calendar day: eight
days. On the held-out window the date is then missed for 2.5\% of delivered
orders against 7.4\%, for an average quoted wait of 18.6 days against 17.5; a
6.9-day margin, set without outcomes to match the platform's average wait,
misses 3.2\%. The padding moves to where the risk is --- Southeast customers, let
down 8.6\% of the time today, are let down 2.4\% --- though dates still missed
are missed by more (6.8 days against 4.4; Appendix~\ref{app:customer}). With
that date no blanket warning is needed; if the platform keeps its own, a warning
at 11\% risk would reach 45\% of buyers, six in seven of them on time, so it
belongs on the riskiest tenth, of whom one in four is late.

\textbf{2. When the promised day ends without a delivery: hear from the platform
first} (customer care; no model). The calendar finds these customers without
false alarms: 69.5 in 1{,}000 orders placed in the test window, 23 of them never
delivered. That day each should get an apology, the parcel's status and a new
date, or a refund where the order will not come. Today, as in Box~1, the survey reaches them
first, typically two days after the promised date, and 77\% of late-parcel
customers who rated 1--2 stars did so before the parcel arrived. The survey
should follow once the order is complete, as it does for parcels on time. Only
the timing changes: every customer is still asked, and no review is filtered,
delayed in publication or rewarded.

\textbf{3. At delivery: help for customers whose parcel came but disappointed}
(customer experience). Across 2017--2018, of the customers whose parcel arrived
and who rated it 1--2 stars, two in three had it on time and wrote a median 2.1
days after it came, about what was in it (Section~\ref{sec:reviews}). Each morning the team
ranks the previous day's deliveries not yet rated and contacts the top of the
list, with a replacement, refund or return to offer: at ten a day, 26 in 100 go
on to rate 1--2 stars, against 8 at random. For orders from several sellers, check
that every item arrived before the survey.

\textbf{4. Over time: dates and lists that stay accurate} (platform). Refit both
models monthly, re-choose the margin at each refit, and track broken promises by
region beside the gap between predicted and realised lead time, the signature of
drift (Section~\ref{sec:results-segments}); a week in which transit lengthens
while the promise does not is the February--March 2018 pattern again
(Appendix~\ref{app:lateness}).

\subsection{Measuring what the business case assumes: a pilot for each contact}
\label{sec:pilot}

Every reais figure above rests on an effect the data cannot show, because the
tables record no contact other than the survey: whether a contact turns a
customer's experience around. A randomised experiment measures it, as the
Phase 2 evaluation asked \citep{kohavi2009controlled}, one arm per moment, each
sized on the customers that moment can still reach (Table~\ref{tab:pilot-arms}).
\emph{Arm B, the promised-date message:} every customer not yet rated when the
promised day ends without a delivery is randomised to the message, with the survey once the order
is complete, or to current practice; a third arm, message but survey unchanged,
separates the two. About 13 a day rated the order only afterwards,
54.7\% of them 1--2 stars: a one-in-four reduction at 80\% power
needs 209 per arm, five weeks for two arms or seven for three. \emph{Arm A, the
delivery-day call:} of each day's top ten delivered, not-yet-rated parcels, five
are contacted. The control rate is 0.260, so the call pays only if it turns
around about one in two; detecting that needs 159 per arm, about five weeks at
ten listed a day. \emph{Outcomes, customer first:} the final 1--2 star rate per
randomised order (not the pre-arrival rate, which holding the survey removes by
construction), the share who review, complaints and refund requests, and
complaints about the contact; then reais per contact. Appendix~\ref{app:pilot}
compares the design on slide 11 (Table~\ref{tab:pilot},
Figure~\ref{fig:pilot}).
